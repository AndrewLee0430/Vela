Vela

繁體中文

Multilingual, citation-first medical search for healthcare professionals who work beyond English.

Built and run from February to October 2026. Vela is now archived: no new accounts, no payments, no new features. A small Research-only demo stays online.

What Vela was

Most clinical evidence is published in English. Vela let a clinician ask a question in their own language, in any of 16, and answered in that language, with each claim tied to a numbered source they could open: a PubMed abstract or an official drug label.

It was a reference tool for professionals, not a symptom checker. It refused questions containing personal identifiers, and it never asked anyone to prove who they were.

Three tools

Research

Ask a clinical question in any of the 16 languages. Vela searched the literature and drug labels, ranked what it found, and wrote a short answer in your language with numbered citations.

Verify

Enter a list of drugs and check them for interactions against the official US drug labels. When the label did not state a severity and the model judged it, the badge said so.

Explain

Paste a lab report. Vela matched each value to its LOINC, RxNorm or MedlinePlus entry and explained it in plain language.

How it worked

Sources
  • PubMed for the literature.
  • US drug labels from DailyMed, indexed by their structured label sections: 4,608 indexed sections from 1,038 labels.[8] openFDA was queried as well, and Verify fell back to it when a label had no interactions section.
  • Taiwan FDA open data, for approved indications and to resolve local brand names to their active ingredients.
  • LOINC, RxNorm and MedlinePlus for Explain.
Stack
A Next.js static export served by a FastAPI backend on Fly.io, with Postgres. Every language-model call went through one provider interface, so models could be swapped and compared on identical inputs.
Honesty guardrails
  • Disclaimers were rendered by the interface; the model was told never to write them.
  • Every answer cited its sources by number. An answer written without any retrieved literature was labelled as such.
  • A severity the model judged, rather than one the label stated, was marked as AI-assessed.
  • An evidence colour the model assigned to its own answers was removed: it was the model grading itself.
  • Questions with personal identifiers were refused before any model saw them. If that check or the safety guards failed, the request was blocked, not let through.

What I measured and learned

Each figure below comes from a file committed to the source repository. The bracketed number points to it.

A cheaper model answered a different question

Asked whether older patients can take blood-pressure medicine with calcium, the smaller model (gpt-4.1-mini) answered about calcium-channel-blocker drugs instead of calcium supplements in 2 of 8 runs. The larger one (gpt-4.1), given the same retrieved documents, did so in 0 of 8. The demo's anonymous Research moved to the larger model.[1]

The right label for the wrong route

All 149 saved answers to 14 questions about supplements and blood-pressure medicines were read and graded. Of those, 42 built advice about an oral supplement from an injection-only label (calcium chloride injection). Every one of the 42 answered the calcium question, in English or Chinese: 42 of its 85 answers, and none of the other 64. The grading was done by reading, not by a clinician. That example question is withheld from the demo.[2]

One drug, two names, two conclusions

Drug labels were indexed under the drug name as written. Aspirin's reference label under ASPIRIN had none of the safety sections the index used; under ACETYLSALICYLIC ACID it had three: contraindications, drug interactions, and warnings and precautions. A conclusion drawn from the first name alone, that in 6 of 6 cases the drug's own label had no safety section, was wrong, and it survived five successive rounds of working notes.[3]

A ranking test that could not see its own documents

A sweep that reweighted retrieval sources showed no change, which read as reassurance. The local tier it reweighted held 690 empty stub documents; in one test query, 11 of the 13 retrieved documents came from it. The local tier was switched off, and ranking changes since then were checked by reading the documents, not only their positions.[4][5]

Text heuristics over drug labels overcount

A text search for veterinary labels flagged 69; checking each label's document-type code left 3 of 1,036. A route-and-form classifier reported 22%; the measured share was 13%. Where a label carries an exact key, Vela used the key, not the words.[6][7]

Why it is archived

Vela answered medical questions with a language model. Offering that to the public runs into a regulatory gate for software that gives medical answers, and clearing it responsibly is not something one person can do on the side. Keeping the answers honest also took constant maintenance: every model change, data source and safety check had to be measured again by hand, as the findings above show. Meanwhile, well-resourced teams now offer capable medical search for free. Rather than let Vela decay while still charging for it, I stopped development, ended subscriptions, and left a small, cost-capped Research demo online as a record of what it did.
