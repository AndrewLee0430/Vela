## Summary

You should generally avoid taking potassium supplements or potassium-containing salt substitutes with blood pressure (BP) medications such as angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs, e.g., valsartan, losartan) or potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g., amiloride), unless specifically directed and closely monitored by your healthcare provider. Combining these can increase your risk of hyperkalemia (high blood potassium), which can be dangerous[1][2][3][4]. The main mechanism is that these BP medications can reduce potassium excretion by the kidneys, leading to potassium retention[1][2][3][4].

---

## Clinical Notes

- **Do NOT use potassium supplements or potassium-sparing diuretics together with ARBs (e.g., valsartan, losartan) unless there is a clear indication and close monitoring, as this combination can cause severe hyperkalemia[1][2][3][4].**
- **Key contraindications:** Avoid potassium supplementation if you have impaired kidney function, are elderly, or are volume-depleted, as these conditions further increase the risk of hyperkalemia and renal impairment[2][4].
- **Drug interactions:** 
    - Potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g., amiloride) and ARBs both increase serum potassium; their combination with potassium supplements is especially risky[1][2][3][4].
    - NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) can further increase potassium retention and reduce the effectiveness of BP medications, as well as worsen renal function[1][2][3][4].
    - Lithium toxicity risk is increased when combined with ARBs or potassium-sparing diuretics[2][3][4].
- **Monitoring parameters:**
    - Check serum potassium and renal function (serum creatinine, eGFR) at baseline and periodically during therapy, especially after starting or changing doses of any interacting medications[1][2][3][4].
    - Frequency: Monitor potassium more frequently (e.g., within 1–2 weeks of starting or changing therapy, then periodically) if any risk factors or interacting drugs are present[1][2][3][4].
    - If serum potassium rises above the upper limit of normal (typically >5.0 mmol/L), reduce or stop potassium supplementation and consider adjusting BP medications[1][2][3][4].
    - Stop potassium supplementation if eGFR drops significantly or if hyperkalemia develops[1][2][3][4].
- **Summary of risks:** Hyperkalemia can cause cardiac arrhythmias and muscle weakness, and is potentially life-threatening. Renal impairment may also occur, especially with NSAID co-use[2][3][4].

If you are considering potassium supplementation, consult your healthcare provider for individualized assessment and monitoring.
