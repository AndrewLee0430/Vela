## Summary  
In elderly patients, daily low-dose aspirin (100 mg) does not significantly affect blood pressure (BP) when used alone or in combination with antihypertensive medications. Longitudinal data from the ASPREE trial showed no meaningful difference in systolic or diastolic BP changes between aspirin and placebo groups over nearly 5 years, regardless of baseline BP or antihypertensive treatment status [1]. Aspirin’s cardiovascular benefits in older adults appear independent of BP modulation, and its interaction with BP medications does not alter BP control significantly [2].

## Clinical Notes  
- **Safety and Use:** Low-dose aspirin (100 mg daily) is generally safe for elderly patients without cardiovascular disease, dementia, or severe physical disability, as studied in community-dwelling older adults [1].  
- **Contraindications:** Aspirin should be used cautiously or avoided in patients with active bleeding, aspirin allergy, or high bleeding risk. No specific contraindications related to BP or antihypertensive drugs were identified in the elderly population studied.  
- **Blood Pressure Effects:** Aspirin does not significantly raise or lower BP in older adults, including those on antihypertensive therapy [1,2]. Higher aspirin doses (not low-dose) may negatively impact renal function and BP control via cyclooxygenase-2 inhibition, but this was not observed with low-dose aspirin [2].  
- **Monitoring:** Routine BP monitoring remains essential in elderly patients on antihypertensive drugs, but aspirin use does not necessitate additional BP monitoring beyond standard care [1]. Renal function monitoring is advisable if higher aspirin doses are used or if there is pre-existing kidney disease, although this is not typical for low-dose aspirin in primary prevention [2].  
- **Clinical Context:** The evidence is derived from large randomized trials in community-dwelling older adults in Australia and the U.S. without prior cardiovascular disease, so caution is warranted when extrapolating to other populations or those with established CVD [1].  
- **Additional Considerations:** Aspirin’s cardiovascular protective effects may involve anti-inflammatory and vascular mechanisms beyond BP modulation, which supports its use alongside antihypertensive therapy without expecting BP changes [2].  

In summary, low-dose aspirin can be safely combined with BP medications in elderly patients without expecting significant BP alterations, but individual bleeding risk and renal function should guide therapy decisions. Regular BP and clinical monitoring remain standard practice.
