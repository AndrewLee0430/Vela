## Summary

Elderly patients can take blood pressure (BP) medications with calcium supplements, but caution is needed with certain drug classes. Specifically, concomitant use of calcium (especially intravenous calcium chloride) with calcium channel blockers should be avoided if possible, as calcium may reduce the antihypertensive effect of these medications; if unavoidable, close blood pressure monitoring is required during administration[1]. Oral calcium citrate supplementation in elderly osteopenic subjects was associated with a decrease in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, not an increase[2].

---

## Clinical Notes

- Avoid concomitant use of intravenous calcium chloride with calcium channel blockers (e.g., amlodipine, diltiazem, verapamil) because calcium may reduce the response to these antihypertensive agents; if co-administration is unavoidable, monitor blood pressure closely during administration[1].
- There is no evidence in the provided context that oral calcium citrate supplementation adversely affects blood pressure control; in fact, a study in elderly osteopenic subjects showed a decrease in both systolic (-2.8 ± 13.9 mmHg, p = 0.0102) and diastolic (-2.1 ± 10.4 mmHg, p = 0.0116) blood pressure from baseline to follow-up[2].
- Monitor for potential hypercalcemia, especially if the patient is taking other drugs that increase calcium levels (e.g., thiazide diuretics, vitamin D, lithium); increase the frequency of serum calcium monitoring in these cases[1].
- For patients on digoxin, avoid concomitant use with intravenous calcium chloride due to the risk of synergistic arrhythmias and increased digoxin toxicity from hypercalcemia; if unavoidable, monitor ECG closely during administration[1].
- The safety profile of oral calcium citrate in elderly patients was favorable, with a low rate of adverse reactions (3.9%, mostly gastrointestinal, none serious)[2].
- No specific contraindications to oral calcium citrate with BP medications were identified in the provided evidence, but always assess for individual drug interactions and comorbidities.
- The evidence for oral calcium citrate is from a 2024 study in elderly Caucasian osteopenic subjects; applicability to other populations or with other forms of calcium supplementation is not addressed in the provided context[2].
