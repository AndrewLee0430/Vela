## Summary

Grapefruit juice significantly increases the bioavailability of several blood pressure (BP) medications, especially certain calcium channel blockers (CCBs) such as felodipine, by inhibiting intestinal CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, leading to higher serum drug concentrations and potentially increased risk of adverse effects [1][2][3][4][5]. This interaction is most clinically relevant for dihydropyridine CCBs (e.g., felodipine, nifedipine), and can occur even after a single glass of grapefruit juice [1][3][4].

---

## Clinical Notes

- **Key BP Medications Affected:** The interaction is most pronounced with 1,4-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, especially felodipine, but also potentially affects other dihydropyridines (e.g., nifedipine) and verapamil [1][2][3][5]. Other antihypertensives are not specifically mentioned in the provided context.
- **Mechanism:** Grapefruit juice inhibits intestinal CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, reducing presystemic metabolism and increasing absorption of susceptible drugs [1][2][3][4][5].
- **Magnitude and Duration:** A single glass of grapefruit juice can produce maximal interaction, and repeated consumption can cause cumulative increases in drug exposure [3][4]. The effect can last up to 24 hours [3].
- **Clinical Risks:** Increased drug concentrations may lead to dose-dependent adverse effects such as hypotension, headache, flushing, or peripheral edema [2][3][4].
- **Contraindications/Warnings:** Avoid grapefruit juice in patients taking affected CCBs, especially those with a history of drug sensitivity or at risk for hypotension [1][2][3][4][5].
- **Monitoring:** Monitor for signs of excessive drug effect (e.g., symptomatic hypotension, bradycardia) in patients who may have consumed grapefruit juice while on susceptible CCBs. No specific laboratory threshold is provided in the context, but clinical vigilance is advised [2][4].
- **Patient Counseling:** Advise patients prescribed affected CCBs to avoid grapefruit juice and grapefruit-containing products entirely [1][2][3][4][5].
- **Uncertainties:** The clinical significance and magnitude of the interaction can vary based on individual patient susceptibility, the type and amount of grapefruit juice, and specific drug characteristics [3][5]. The context does not provide data on non-CCB antihypertensives.

If considering BP medications for a patient who regularly consumes grapefruit juice, select agents not metabolized by CYP3A4 or not known to interact, as per the above evidence.
