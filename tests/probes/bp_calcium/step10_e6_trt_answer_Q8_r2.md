## Summary

Placebo (such as sugar pills) can lead to a significant reduction in clinic-measured blood pressure in patients with hypertension, with reported mean systolic blood pressure reductions of approximately 5.9 mm Hg in nonresistant hypertension and 8.8 mm Hg in resistant hypertension placebo arms of modern trials [2]. However, this effect is largely an artifact of clinic blood pressure measurement and is not observed with ambulatory intra-arterial monitoring, indicating that the true physiological effect of placebo is minimal [1]. The apparent blood pressure reduction with placebo highlights the importance of using placebo controls in antihypertensive drug trials to accurately assess the efficacy of active medications [1], [2].

---

## Clinical Notes

- Placebo (sugar pills) does not have a true antihypertensive effect when assessed by ambulatory intra-arterial monitoring; observed reductions in clinic blood pressure are likely due to measurement artifacts or regression to the mean rather than a physiological effect [1].
- In clinical trials, the use of placebo controls is essential for quantifying the true effect of antihypertensive medications, but placebo should not be used as a substitute for active therapy in patients with moderate to severe hypertension or those at high cardiovascular risk due to ethical concerns [3].
- Placebo-controlled trials should minimize patient exposure to placebo, especially in those with severe hypertension or major risk factors, and should exclude such high-risk individuals from placebo arms whenever possible [3].
- Monitoring parameters: In research settings, both clinic and ambulatory blood pressure should be measured to distinguish true drug effects from placebo artifacts [1], [2]. In clinical practice, do not rely on placebo for blood pressure management.
- Contraindications: Placebo should not be used as a therapeutic intervention for hypertension outside of controlled research settings, particularly in patients with established cardiovascular disease, severe hypertension, or other significant comorbidities [3].
- Safety: There is a risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes if placebo is used in place of effective antihypertensive therapy in high-risk patients [3]. 

The evidence base includes data from both older (1981) and more recent (2015) studies, with consistent findings regarding the lack of true antihypertensive effect from placebo and the importance of rigorous trial design [1], [2].
