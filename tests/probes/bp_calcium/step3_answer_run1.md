## Summary

Elderly patients can take blood pressure medications that include calcium channel blockers, as these agents are specifically noted to be effective in this population and may be preferred in certain clinical scenarios[2]. The main antihypertensive mechanism is through reduction of systemic vascular resistance, and calcium channel blockers have demonstrated efficacy in elderly patients, particularly those with early end-organ damage or specific comorbidities[2].

## Clinical Notes

- Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) are considered effective antihypertensive agents for elderly patients, especially those with left ventricular hypertrophy or renal insufficiency[2].
- CCBs may be preferred in elderly or Black patients, or in individuals whose lifestyle involves intense physical or mental exertion, as they maintain hemodynamics better than beta-blockers[2].
- Non-dihydropyridine CCBs (e.g., verapamil, diltiazem) are also used for supraventricular arrhythmias, but caution is advised in patients with arrhythmias such as Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome due to the risk of provoking anterograde conduction[2].
- Drug-drug interactions are a concern in elderly hypertensive patients, who often require multiple medications. Calcium channel blockers, particularly non-dihydropyridines, are implicated in clinically relevant pharmacokinetic interactions, mainly via the cytochrome P450 system or P-glycoprotein[1].
- Monitoring for drug interactions is important, especially when CCBs are combined with other antihypertensives or medications metabolized by CYP450 enzymes[1].
- The evidence provided does not specify concrete monitoring parameters or thresholds for elderly patients on CCBs, nor does it detail absolute contraindications or frequency of monitoring.
- The context does not address the use of calcium supplements or dietary calcium in combination with antihypertensive therapy, so no conclusion can be drawn about interactions with calcium itself.
