## Summary

Yes, elderly patients can take blood pressure medications with calcium, but caution is required due to potential drug interactions and the risk of hypercalcemia. Calcium channel blockers are effective and generally well tolerated in elderly hypertensive patients, but concomitant use of calcium supplements or calcium chloride injections may reduce the antihypertensive effect and increase the risk of adverse events, especially when combined with certain medications such as digoxin or thiazide diuretics[1][2][3][4].

---

## Clinical Notes

- **Drug Interactions:** Concomitant use of calcium chloride injection with calcium channel blockers may reduce the antihypertensive response to these agents; avoid this combination if possible, or monitor blood pressure closely if unavoidable[1].
- **Digoxin:** Avoid using calcium chloride injection with digoxin due to the risk of synergistic arrhythmias and increased digoxin toxicity from hypercalcemia. If unavoidable, monitor ECG closely during administration[1].
- **Hypercalcemia Risk:** Calcium chloride injection, when used with other drugs that increase calcium levels (e.g., thiazide diuretics, vitamin D, lithium), raises the risk of hypercalcemia. Increase the frequency of serum calcium monitoring in these patients[1].
- **Monitoring Parameters:** 
    - Monitor blood pressure regularly when combining calcium supplements or calcium chloride with antihypertensive therapy, especially calcium channel blockers[1].
    - Monitor serum calcium concentrations more frequently if the patient is on multiple agents that can increase calcium levels[1].
    - Monitor ECG if digoxin is also prescribed[1].
- **Contraindications and Cautions:** 
    - Avoid calcium chloride injection in patients on digoxin or calcium channel blockers unless absolutely necessary[1].
    - Use caution in elderly patients with comorbidities that predispose to hypercalcemia or arrhythmias[1].
- **Efficacy and Safety:** Calcium channel blockers are effective and generally safe for elderly hypertensive patients, with favorable metabolic profiles and no evidence of increased mortality or cancer risk in large trials[2][3][4]. However, the evidence base for some recommendations is older (pre-2000) and may not reflect the latest clinical practice standards[2][4][5].
- **Population Applicability:** The cited studies primarily involve elderly patients in Western populations; applicability to other ethnicities or regions is not addressed in the provided context.

In summary, elderly patients can take blood pressure medications with calcium, but careful attention to drug interactions, monitoring, and individual risk factors is essential[1][2][3][4].
