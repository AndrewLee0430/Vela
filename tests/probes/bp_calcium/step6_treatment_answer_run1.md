## Summary

Elderly patients can take blood pressure (BP) medications with calcium supplements, but caution is needed if the calcium is administered intravenously as calcium chloride. Oral calcium citrate supplementation was not associated with increased adverse events or blood pressure elevation in elderly osteopenic subjects; in fact, both systolic and diastolic blood pressure decreased modestly during supplementation (mean change -2.8 mmHg systolic, -2.1 mmHg diastolic) [2]. However, intravenous calcium chloride may reduce the response to calcium channel blockers, so concomitant use should be avoided or closely monitored [1].

## Clinical Notes

- **Oral Calcium Citrate:** In elderly osteopenic patients, daily oral calcium citrate (500 mg) was well tolerated, with a low rate of adverse reactions (3.9%, mainly gastrointestinal) and no serious adverse events reported over one year [2]. No evidence of blood pressure elevation was observed; instead, a modest decrease in both systolic and diastolic BP was reported [2].
- **Intravenous Calcium Chloride:** Concomitant use of intravenous calcium chloride and calcium channel blockers may reduce the antihypertensive effect of the latter. If unavoidable, monitor blood pressure closely during administration [1].
- **Other Drug Interactions:** Calcium chloride should be avoided with digoxin due to risk of synergistic arrhythmias and increased digoxin toxicity; if used, close ECG monitoring is required [1].
- **Hypercalcemia Risk:** For patients on drugs that increase hypercalcemia risk (e.g., thiazide diuretics, vitamin D, lithium), increase the frequency of serum calcium monitoring when combined with calcium supplements [1].
- **Monitoring:** For oral calcium supplementation, routine monitoring of serum calcium is prudent in patients at risk for hypercalcemia or on interacting medications. For intravenous calcium, monitor ECG (if on digoxin) and blood pressure (if on calcium channel blockers) during administration [1].
- **Contraindications:** Avoid intravenous calcium chloride in patients with ventricular fibrillation or hypercalcemia [1]. No specific contraindications for oral calcium citrate were reported in the elderly osteopenic cohort studied [2].

No evidence was provided regarding the interaction of oral calcium with specific classes of BP medications other than calcium channel blockers, and the cited study population was elderly, predominantly female, and Caucasian [2]. Data may not generalize to other populations or to other forms of calcium supplementation.
