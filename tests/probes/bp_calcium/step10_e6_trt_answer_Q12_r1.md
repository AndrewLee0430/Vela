## Summary

Spironolactone increases serum potassium levels and can cause hyperkalemia, especially when combined with other agents that raise potassium or in patients with comorbidities. In gender-diverse adolescents without other medical issues, the risk of clinically significant hyperkalemia is low, transient, and typically asymptomatic [1], [4]. In heart failure patients, hyperkalemia is a common barrier to optimal spironolactone dosing, but potassium-lowering agents like sodium zirconium cyclosilicate can help maintain normokalemia during therapy [5].

## Clinical Notes

- **Safety Warnings and Contraindications:**
  - Do not use spironolactone with potassium supplements or other potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g., amiloride, eplerenone) due to risk of severe hyperkalemia [1], [2], [3].
  - Avoid concomitant use with drugs that increase serum potassium, such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs, heparin, trimethoprim, cyclosporine, and tacrolimus, unless there is a compelling indication and close monitoring is feasible [1], [2], [3].
  - Avoid use with mitotane [1].
  - Use caution in patients with renal impairment, as reduced renal function increases the risk of hyperkalemia [1].

- **Drug Interactions:**
  - Lithium: Spironolactone reduces renal clearance of lithium, increasing risk of lithium toxicity; monitor lithium levels if used together [1], [3].
  - NSAIDs: May reduce the diuretic, natriuretic, and antihypertensive effects of spironolactone and increase potassium; monitor blood pressure, renal function, and potassium [1], [2], [3].
  - Cholestyramine: Hyperkalemic metabolic acidosis has been reported with concomitant use [1].
  - Digoxin: Spironolactone can interfere with digoxin assays [1].

- **Monitoring Parameters:**
  - Check serum potassium and renal function at baseline and regularly during therapy. In heart failure or when combined with other potassium-raising agents, monitor potassium more frequently (e.g., within 1 week of initiation or dose change, then monthly for the first 3 months, then every 3 months) [1].
  - In gender-diverse adolescents without comorbidities, hyperkalemia is rare and transient, but initial monitoring is still recommended; frequency may be reduced if stable [4].
  - If serum potassium >5.0 mmol/L, consider dose reduction or discontinuation of spironolactone [1], [4].
  - In heart failure patients, potassium-lowering agents (e.g., sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) can be used to enable higher spironolactone doses while maintaining normokalemia [5].

- **Thresholds:**
  - Discontinue or reduce dose if serum potassium exceeds 5.0–5.5 mmol/L, depending on clinical context and comorbidities [1], [4].
  - Avoid spironolactone if baseline potassium is elevated or if eGFR is significantly reduced (<30 mL/min/1.73 m²) [1].

- **Special Populations:**
  - In adolescents using spironolactone for gender transition, hyperkalemia is rare, transient, and asymptomatic, with no clear relationship to dose or duration [4].
  - In heart failure, hyperkalemia is a frequent limiting factor for spironolactone use; potassium binders may facilitate optimal dosing [5].
